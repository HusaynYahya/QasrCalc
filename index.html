          The rulings in brief

          What counts as a journey

          Three conditions must be met before a person is a traveller in the eyes of the law:

          1. The legal distance. Eight farsakh — 44 km, near enough 27½ miles — counted along the path actually travelled, not as a straight line on the map. The outward and return legs are added together, so 22 km each way is enough if you return without staying ten days; and it does not matter how the total divides between them. Twelve miles out and sixteen back is the same journey, in law, as fourteen and fourteen.
          2. The intention from the outset. One must intend to cover the distance when setting out. A person who leaves looking for a petrol station is no traveller, even if he ends up 200 km away — though from the moment he decides to continue, the distance is counted afresh from there. One compelled to travel, such as onto a train that does not stop before the limit, is a traveller.
          3. Nothing interrupting the journey. See below.

          What interrupts a journey

          • Reaching one of your hometowns and stopping in it — the distance then restarts from that town.
          • Intending to stay ten continuous days in one place — you then pray as a resident there.
          • Remaining undecided in one place for thirty days — after the thirtieth day you become a resident.

          What a hometown is

          A watan in the language of the law is not simply where you own a house. Owning property is not the criterion; residing somewhere long enough that you are no longer counted a traveller is. A place is your hometown if it is where you belong (usually your birthplace and your parents' home), the place you have chosen to spend the rest of your life, a place adopted for a long residence — for study or work — or a place you return to regularly for a significant period, such as an ancestral village each summer. A person may have more than one hometown, and prays full in each.

          Which road you take

          The distance is the road you actually travel, so the choice of route can decide the matter. Where the direct road is 26 miles and the scenic one 30, the first journey is prayed in full and the second shortened — same two towns, different rulings. Choose the road you mean to drive, not the shortest one on the map.

          Two lines, not one

          It is easy to run together two boundaries which the law keeps apart.

          • Where the distance is counted from: the city border. The measurement starts once you have travelled far enough to be considered a traveller by common reckoning — in practice, the edge of your city. Not your front door.
          • Where the distance is counted to: the destination itself. Not the destination's city border, and not its hadd al-tarakhkhus — the actual place you are going.
          • Where the shortening begins: the hadd al-tarakhkhus. The point at which the people of the town can no longer see you, nor you them — a little beyond the border. Reach the city border at Dhuhr and you still pray in full; it is not yet a shortened prayer.
          • Where the shortening ends: the city border again. Coming home, keep shortening until you are within your city border. Reaching the hadd al-tarakhkhus is not enough on the return.

          The same distinction governs the fast, in both directions. On the way out you may not break a fast at the city border — wait until you have crossed the hadd al-tarakhkhus. On the way home, to keep a fast you must be inside the city border before the adhan of Dhuhr; the hadd al-tarakhkhus will not do.

          Leaving a place where you stayed ten days is different again: there the hadd al-tarakhkhus has no bearing, and you shorten as soon as you leave the town itself.

          A place you have only just moved to

          Somewhere adopted for a long stay — a student arriving for five years — is not yet a hometown on the first day, and a traveller who holds no intention of ten continuous days is not a resident either. By obligatory precaution, one prays both: the four-rak'ah prayers shortened, and again in full. Once you have lived there long enough that people no longer count you a traveller, it is your hometown and you pray in full — even if you are only there to study, and do not mean to stay for life.

          Prayer and fasting

          • The four-rak'ah prayers — Dhuhr, Asr and Isha — become two rak'ahs. Maghrib remains three and Fajr two.
          • The rulings begin at the hadd al-tarakhkhus, not at the city border. See Two lines, not one above.
          • A traveller does not fast. If one is fasting and sets out after the adhan of Dhuhr, that day's fast must be completed; if one sets out before it, the fast is not valid and is made up later.

          Where the rulings do not apply

          A journey undertaken for a sinful purpose; a person whose occupation involves constant travel, such as a driver or a commuting student; a nomad who wanders; and one who has no permanent home and is always travelling — all of these pray in full.

          The four places of choice

          In Makkah, Madinah, the Masjid of Kufa and the sanctuary of Imam al-Husayn (peace be upon him), a traveller may choose between shortening and praying in full.
